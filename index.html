12.1 · How Did Our Modern Agriculture Develop?

From Hunter-Gatherers to Agriculture

  • Until ~10,000 years ago, humans were nomadic hunter-gatherers.
  • Advantages: Varied diet. Fewer “work” hours.
  • Disadvantages: Violence. Predation.
  • Agriculture enabled larger populations on less land. Crops (wheat, corn) and animals (cattle, chickens, pigs) were domesticated.
  • ~6,000 years ago: Egyptian and Mesopotamian irrigation; Chinese organic fertilization with plant and animal waste.
  • The Roman Empire rotated crops and used fallow fields to restore soil. Native Americans enriched soil with fish and used companion planting.

12.1 · The Green Revolution

Tripling Yields in 40 Years

  • Gas- and diesel-powered equipment replaced human and animal power, reducing labor but increasing fossil fuel dependence.
  • Large-scale irrigation, synthetic pesticides, and synthetic fertilizers were adopted globally after 1960.
  • Average grain yields tripled to 6 metric tons per hectare within just 40 years.
  • Genetic engineering (1970s) created GMOs, now 88% of US corn and 94% of soybeans are genetically modified.

12.1 · Synthetic Pesticides and Fertilizers

Two Agricultural Inputs with Outsized Environmental Consequences

Synthetic Pesticides
  • 70–100 of the top food crops rely on pollinators and pesticides harm them.
  • Overuse creates chemical resistance, requiring ever-stronger applications over time.
  • CDC: 20,000 of 2 million US farmworkers suffer pesticide poisoning each year.
  • Toxic bioaccumulation affects species above insects in the food chain.
Synthetic Fertilizers
  • Global use grew from 14M tons (1950) to 177M+ tons today.
  • Degrade soil health; run off into waterways harming aquatic life.
  • Soil microbes convert nitrogen fertilizers into N₂O, 300× more potent than CO₂.
  • Primary driver of eutrophication and dead zones in rivers and coastal oceans.

12.2 · How Does Modern Agriculture Impact the Environment?

Loss of Agrodiversity

  • The Green Revolution selected certain crop varieties for production over others and reduced agrobiodiversity.
  • Loss of crop varieties leads to genetic erosion.
  • The resultant crop or livestock monoculture temporarily have higher yields but are more vulnerable to pests and diseases.

Where Did Our Food Come From?

True or false? Answer each one on your laptop. The instructor reveals them one at a time.

  1. 1
    Wild almonds are poisonous.

    Wild almond kernels make amygdalin, a bitter compound that releases cyanide. A mutation in a single gene switched it off, and early farmers kept the rare sweet trees.

    Sánchez-Pérez et al. (2019), “Mutation of a bHLH transcription factor allowed almond domestication,” Science 364:1095–1098.

  2. 2
    Wild bananas are packed with seeds.

    Bananas went from wild to edible through seed suppression and parthenocarpy: fruit that develops without seeds. Today’s bananas are sterile, so new plants are grown from shoots of old ones.

    Perrier et al. (2011), “Multidisciplinary perspectives on banana (Musa spp.) domestication,” PNAS 108:11311–11318; Heslop-Harrison & Schwarzacher (2007), Annals of Botany 100:1073–1084.

  3. 3
    There are over 4,000 edible varieties of potato.

    Most of them grow in the Andes of South America, the potato’s original home.

    International Potato Center (CIP), “Potato: facts and figures,” cipotato.org/potato.

  4. 4
    Broccoli, cabbage, kale, cauliflower and Brussels sprouts are all one species.

    All are Brassica oleracea, bred from one wild Mediterranean plant for different parts: leaves (kale, cabbage), flower buds (broccoli, cauliflower) and side buds (Brussels sprouts).

    Mabry et al. (2021), “The evolutionary history of wild, domesticated, and feral Brassica oleracea,” Molecular Biology and Evolution 38:4419–4434.

  5. 5
    Corn’s wild ancestor grew only about a dozen kernels per ear.

    Teosinte, a wild grass from Mexico, holds roughly ten to twelve kernels, each sealed in a stone-hard case. A change in one gene, tga1, helped expose the naked kernels we eat today.

    Doebley (2004), “The genetics of maize evolution,” Annual Review of Genetics 38:37–59; Wang et al. (2005), “The origin of the naked grains of maize,” Nature 436:714–719.

12.4 · Is Conventional Meat Production Sustainable?

Is Conventional Meat Production Sustainable?

Manure & Pollution
CAFOs produce 1.3B tons of manure/yr, which is 40× US human sewage. Runoff can pollute groundwater with pathogens like Salmonella.
Antibiotic Resistance
Broad CAFO antibiotic use drives resistance to penicillin and tetracycline, identified by WHO as a major global health threat.
Air Quality
Organic dust, methane, and ammonia from CAFO operations create serious air pollution for surrounding communities.
Foodborne Illness
Raw meat causes 48 million foodborne illness cases per year in the US, driven by CAFO-scale production conditions.

12.5 · How Have Our Food Systems Changed?

The Food-Water-Energy Nexus

The food-water-energy nexus is the interconnected relationship among food, water, and energy resources.

Water⇄Food

Farms take about 72% of the world’s freshwater withdrawals, and fertilizer that runs off the fields returns to rivers and coasts as pollution.

Energy⇄Food

Food systems use about 30% of the world’s energy. Making nitrogen fertilizer alone takes about 2%, and about a third of the US corn harvest becomes fuel ethanol.

Water⇄Energy

Cooling power plants accounts for 41% of all US water withdrawals, and pumping, treating and moving water takes energy in return.

FAO AQUASTAT (2025); FAO, Energy-Smart Food for People and Climate (2011); IEA, Ammonia Technology Roadmap (2021); USDA WASDE, 2024/25 corn; USGS Fact Sheet 2018-3035 (2015 data).

12.5 · Changing Diets and Overnourishment

What Changed in the Modern Diet?

More Sugar, Fat & Salt
Compared to 100 years ago: 60% more sugar, 2× fat, 3.4× salt. Processed foods optimized for convenience while reducing fiber.
Overnourishment
More people are now overweight or obese globally than suffer from hunger. US obesity rose from 15% to 33% since 1980.
Food Security
Affordable access to sufficient nutritious food; threatened by declining agrobiodiversity and extreme weather events.

12.6 · How Do Our Food Choices Link to Sustainability?

Eating Lower on the Food Chain

What it takes to put the same 1,000 Calories on your plate

Every plate holds the same 1,000 Calories. Beans and potatoes are eaten directly, so the Calories grown are the Calories eaten. A chicken turns about 13% of its feed Calories into food, and beef cattle about 3%, so 1,000 Calories of beef takes about 34,500 Calories of feed and about 2,700 gallons of water: roughly 22 times the water of potatoes. Energy is lost at each step up the food chain.

Water: global average water footprint per Calorie, green + blue + grey (Mekonnen & Hoekstra 2012, Ecosystems; beans = pulses, potatoes = starchy roots). Most of beef’s water is rain on pasture and feed crops. Feed: US feed-to-food Calorie conversion, beef 2.9% ± 0.7, poultry 13% ± 4 (Shepon et al. 2016, Environmental Research Letters); much of beef’s feed is pasture and hay that people cannot eat.

12.6 · Organic, Fair Trade, and Local Food

Alternatives to Industrial Agriculture

Organic Agriculture
No synthetic pesticides, GMOs, or hormones. Requires crop rotation and organic soil amendments. Certified through federal inspection.
Fair Trade
Assures adequate wages and sustainable production. 75% of hungry people are farmers; fair trade directly supports them.
Buying Local / CSA
Consumer-supported agriculture subscriptions reduce food miles and support community food systems and regional resilience.
